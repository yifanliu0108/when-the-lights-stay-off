The question

Introduction

Do severe-weather outages tend to last longer than equipment-failure outages?

A brief interruption and a multi-day outage require very different preparation. This report examines 1,534 recorded major outage events from the continental United States in the Purdue LASCI dataset. Each row describes a recorded event affecting a state. These records do not represent every household interruption.

The main columns are CAUSE.CATEGORY (reported cause) and OUTAGE.DURATION (minutes). The start and restoration date/time fields describe event timing. State and starting month provide the baseline model's predictors.

Testing the planned comparison

Hypothesis Testing

Null: The reported-duration distributions are identical for severe-weather and equipment-failure events. Cause labels are exchangeable under this null, with an expected difference of zero in mean log(1 + duration in minutes).

Alternative: Severe-weather outages have a larger mean log(1 + duration in minutes).

The statistic is the weather-group mean of log(1 + minutes) minus the equipment-failure-group mean. This reduces the influence of the longest events while retaining zeros. It is a comparison of transformed means, not arithmetic mean minutes or medians.

One-sided permutation test · α = 0.05

2.0826observed difference

None of 10,000 cause-label permutations produced a statistic this large. With the +1 Monte Carlo correction, p = 1/10001 ≈ 0.000100.

At α = 0.05, I reject the null under the permutation assumptions. The records provide evidence of a larger mean log-transformed duration for severe-weather events. This does not establish causation: state and year can confound the association, shared storms can create dependence, and duration reporting may be selective.

Framing a Prediction Problem

I predict OUTAGE.DURATION in minutes at the first outage report. This is regression. State and starting-calendar information are available then; restoration timestamps, duration-derived features, confirmed cause, and final customer or demand-loss totals are excluded.

MAE is the primary metric because it expresses absolute prediction error in minutes and is less dominated by extreme outages than RMSE. RMSE is a secondary measure of the effect of large errors. Missing targets are excluded, and valid zeros remain.

A first predictive model

Baseline Model

The baseline is a shallow decision-tree regressor using two original inputs: U.S._STATE and MONTH. Both are treated as nominal categories, with no quantitative or ordinal inputs. One sklearn pipeline applies training-mode imputation, one-hot encoding with unknown categories ignored, and an absolute-error tree with maximum depth 3 and minimum leaf size 20.

I trained on 1,219 events beginning before January 1, 2014, and evaluated on 257 events beginning in 2014–July 2016. All preprocessing is fitted on training records only. A median-only reference always predicts the training median of 900 minutes.

The baseline improves MAE by only 0.42% over the reference. An average absolute error of about 36.0 hours is a weak starting point, and the much larger RMSE shows that some long outages remain difficult to predict. The current model is not ready for operational use.

Final Model

The next experiment will add onset hour and weekday and use cyclic encodings for seasonal timing. I plan to try a random-forest regressor and tune depth, minimum leaf size, and number of trees using chronological cross-validation on training records only. The final comparison will use the same holdout. No final-model improvement has been established yet.
