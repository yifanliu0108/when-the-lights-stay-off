Introduction

My question is: do severe-weather outages tend to last longer than equipment-failure outages?

I am using the Purdue power-outages dataset. It has 1,534 major U.S. outage records from January 2000 to July 2016. Long outages can affect people's daily lives, so I wanted to look at how duration differs by cause.

The main columns are CAUSE.CATEGORY (reported cause) and OUTAGE.DURATION (duration in minutes). I also use U.S._STATE and MONTH to predict duration.

Data Cleaning and Exploratory Data Analysis

I skipped the description and units rows in the Excel file and removed an empty column. I combined the date and time columns into start and restoration timestamps. I kept zero durations and left missing values as missing.

There are 58 missing durations. I exclude these records when duration is needed. In 31 records, the reported duration differs from the timestamp interval by more than one minute. I kept the reported duration instead of replacing it.

Hypothesis Testing

Null hypothesis: Duration has the same distribution for severe-weather and equipment-failure outages.

Alternative hypothesis: Severe-weather outages have a larger mean log(1 + duration in minutes).

My statistic is the weather-group mean log(1 + duration) minus the equipment-failure-group mean. I used a one-sided permutation test with 10,000 shuffles and a significance level of 0.05. Missing durations were excluded, and zeros were kept.

The observed statistic was 2.082619. None of the shuffled statistics was as large, giving a corrected p-value of 1/10001, or about 0.000100. I reject the null under the test's assumptions. This supports an association with higher mean log-transformed duration for weather outages, but it does not show causation. Differences in location, year, or reporting could also matter.

Framing a Prediction Problem

I want to predict OUTAGE.DURATION in minutes when an outage is first reported. This is a regression problem. I use information known at the start, such as state and month. I leave out restoration information, confirmed cause, and final impact totals.

I use MAE because it measures absolute prediction error in minutes. I also report RMSE to show the effect of large errors.

Baseline Model

My baseline is a decision-tree regressor using state and starting month. Both inputs are treated as nominal categories. One sklearn Pipeline fills missing inputs with the training mode, applies one-hot encoding, and fits the tree. The tree uses absolute-error splits, a maximum depth of 3, and at least 20 training records per leaf.

I trained on 1,219 records from 2000-2013 and tested on 257 records from 2014-July 2016. All preprocessing was fitted on the training data only. I also compared the tree with a model that always predicts the training median of 900 minutes.
